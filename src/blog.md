<h1 id="the-global-digital-compact-one-year-on">The Global Digital Compact One Year On: Promises, Progress, and Persistent Gaps</h1>

<span class="blog-date">12 May 2026 · Patryk Pawlak</span>

When the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Global Digital Compact (GDC) in September 2024, it was hailed as a generational achievement—a multilateral consensus on the principles and structures needed to govern an increasingly digital world. Eighteen months on, the picture is more complicated. Ambitious commitments have been made; their operationalisation has lagged considerably.

The Compact's five objectives—bridging digital divides, expanding an inclusive digital economy, fostering a safe and trustworthy digital space, advancing responsible AI governance, and strengthening international digital cooperation—each map onto the four dimensions of the Internet Accountability Compass. Tracking progress against them is therefore both a natural extension of the Compass's purpose and a test of the Compact's underlying coherence.

## What the Compact Promised

The GDC represented a decisive shift from voluntary norm-setting to structured accountability. For the first time, a UN-endorsed framework explicitly linked universal connectivity targets to human rights obligations, and placed AI governance within a broader accountability ecosystem. The commitment to establish a Global Digital Compact follow-up mechanism—with national reporting cycles—was particularly significant: it created at least the architecture for accountability, even if the substance of that architecture remains to be filled in.

Crucially, the Compact also acknowledged the infrastructure gap in low- and middle-income countries, committing to mobilise international public and private finance for digital infrastructure. The Compass data shows that this gap is not merely a matter of access: countries with the lowest connectivity scores also cluster at the bottom of the rights and resilience dimensions, suggesting that infrastructure deficits compound digital exclusion in ways that pure connectivity metrics miss.

## Where Implementation Lags

The gap between GDC commitments and measurable implementation is most visible in three areas. First, financing for digital infrastructure has not materialised at the scale or speed promised. Second, the AI governance provisions—while forward-looking—remain vague enough that states are interpreting them in ways that reflect existing national preferences rather than converging on shared standards. Third, the follow-up mechanism itself is still being designed, and early indications suggest that reporting obligations will be light.

For the Compass, these gaps translate into a methodological challenge: how to distinguish between countries that are making genuine progress toward the GDC's objectives and those that are simply reclassifying existing policies as GDC-aligned. The team is currently working on supplementary indicators to address this, which will be integrated into the next edition of the data.

<h1 id="bridging-the-digital-divide">Bridging the Digital Divide: Infrastructure Accountability in the Global South</h1>

<span class="blog-date">28 April 2026 · Nils Berglund</span>

Despite decades of international commitments to universal connectivity, nearly 2.6 billion people remain offline. The gap is not simply technical—it is political, financial, and institutional. Understanding why connectivity targets continue to be missed requires looking beyond bandwidth and tower counts to the accountability structures that govern infrastructure investment and deployment.

The Compass's connectivity and infrastructure dimension tracks not only whether people have access to the Internet, but whether the governance frameworks surrounding that access meet international standards of transparency, inclusion, and long-term planning. Across this dimension, the data reveals a striking pattern: the countries furthest from connectivity targets are also the countries with the weakest accountability frameworks for infrastructure governance.

## The Infrastructure Accountability Deficit

Infrastructure accountability encompasses several interconnected mechanisms: regulatory oversight of telecoms markets, transparency in spectrum allocation, public reporting on universal service fund disbursement, and independent monitoring of rollout commitments. In many low-income countries, one or more of these mechanisms is absent or dysfunctional.

The consequences are well-documented. Without transparent spectrum allocation, dominant operators can sit on unused spectrum while challenger providers are locked out. Without public reporting on universal service funds—often collected as a levy on mobile revenues—governments lack the data to know whether the money collected is actually reaching underserved communities. The Compass data shows that countries scoring poorly on accountability tend to have universal service funds that are either inactive or opaque.

## Bright Spots and Lessons Learned

The picture is not uniformly bleak. Several countries in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia have implemented accountability reforms that are beginning to show results. Rwanda's approach to spectrum management—characterised by transparent auctions, short licence terms, and mandatory coverage obligations with enforceable penalties—is increasingly cited as a model. The Philippines' reform of its Universal Service Fund in 2022, which created an independent oversight board with civil society representation, has improved both disbursement rates and the quality of connectivity data available to policymakers.

These examples share a common feature: accountability was built into the governance framework from the outset, rather than grafted on after the fact. For the Compass, they provide evidence that the correlation between accountability scores and connectivity outcomes is not merely coincidental—it reflects a genuine causal relationship that should inform both the design of international support programmes and the conditionality attached to infrastructure financing.

<h1 id="ai-governance-in-practice">AI Governance in Practice: From Principles to Accountability Frameworks</h1>

<span class="blog-date">14 April 2026 · Patryk Pawlak</span>

The proliferation of AI governance frameworks has been remarkable. Over 60 national AI strategies, more than 160 sets of AI principles, and a growing stack of regulatory instruments now populate the global AI governance landscape. Yet this abundance of policy activity has not translated into a correspondingly clear picture of accountability. Who is responsible when an AI system causes harm? What mechanisms exist to enforce the principles that governments and companies have signed up to? These questions remain mostly unanswered.

The Compass's responsibility and sustainability dimension includes several indicators that touch on AI governance, most notably the Global Index on Responsible AI. Tracking these indicators over time reveals a familiar pattern: strong performance on the input side (strategy adoption, principle endorsement, public investment in AI research) and weak performance on the output side (enforcement capacity, incident reporting, independent auditing).

## The Proliferation of AI Principles

The first wave of AI governance was dominated by principles documents—high-level statements of values such as fairness, transparency, accountability, and safety. These documents served an important agenda-setting function, establishing a common vocabulary and legitimising AI governance as a policy priority. Their limitation was also clear: without enforcement mechanisms or measurable indicators, they were impossible to hold governments and companies to account against.

The second wave—still ongoing—has seen a shift toward regulation. The EU's AI Act is the most prominent example, but it is not alone. Brazil's AI Bill, Canada's Artificial Intelligence and Data Act, and a growing number of sector-specific instruments in Asia and Africa are adding regulatory substance to what were previously voluntary commitments. For the Compass, this shift creates both opportunities and challenges: more law means more benchmarks to track, but also more variation in what "compliance" actually means across jurisdictions.

## Towards Binding Accountability

The third wave—barely beginning—involves the creation of institutional infrastructure for AI accountability: independent audit bodies, mandatory incident registries, liability frameworks for AI-caused harm, and international coordination mechanisms. These institutions are necessary conditions for accountability, but they take time to build and longer still to embed in national governance cultures.

The Compass data suggests that countries investing in accountability institutions now are likely to see improvements across multiple dimensions in the medium term. The correlation between strong institutional capacity scores and good performance on rights and resilience outcomes is one of the most robust findings in the dataset. The implication is clear: AI governance is not just a responsibility and sustainability issue—it is foundational to the entire accountability architecture that the Compass is designed to track.

<h1 id="cybersecurity-as-a-sovereignty-question">Cybersecurity as a Sovereignty Question: How Nations Are Reshaping the Resilience Agenda</h1>

<span class="blog-date">1 April 2026 · Nils Berglund</span>

For much of the Internet's history, cybersecurity was treated primarily as a technical problem. Patches, firewalls, and encryption protocols were the domain of engineers, not diplomats. That era is clearly over. Cybersecurity is now a core dimension of national security strategy, foreign policy, and—increasingly—democratic politics. The question for accountability frameworks is how to track this shift without either overmilitarising the concept or reducing it to a checklist of technical controls.

The Compass's trust and resilience dimension grapples with precisely this challenge. Its indicators span technical capacity (national CERT capabilities, incident response infrastructure), governance quality (national cybersecurity strategies, inter-agency coordination mechanisms), and international engagement (participation in bilateral and multilateral cybersecurity cooperation frameworks). Together, they provide a more complete picture of resilience than any single metric could capture.

## The New Threat Landscape

The threat landscape has shifted dramatically over the past five years. Ransomware attacks on critical infrastructure—hospitals, water utilities, electoral systems—have moved from theoretical concern to documented reality. State-sponsored intrusion campaigns have targeted government networks across every region. And the emergence of AI-assisted cyberattacks has compressed the time available for defenders to detect and respond to intrusions.

Against this backdrop, the Compass data shows wide variation in national preparedness. High-income countries with long-established cybersecurity institutions—particularly in Northern Europe and East Asia—score consistently well. Middle-income countries show the highest variance: some have made rapid progress through targeted investment and international partnerships, while others have strategic documents without the institutional substance to back them up. Low-income countries cluster at the bottom, often lacking even basic incident reporting infrastructure.

## From Defensive Postures to Collective Resilience

The most significant trend in the Compass data is the growing gap between countries that treat cybersecurity as a collective governance challenge and those that treat it as an exclusively national security matter. The former group tends to share threat intelligence, participate actively in multilateral frameworks, and invest in the cybersecurity capacity of their neighbours and partners. The latter group often prioritises surveillance capabilities and content control over genuine resilience, scoring highly on some technical indicators while performing poorly on the governance and international cooperation dimensions.

This divergence has implications for how accountability should be measured. A country with strong surveillance infrastructure and weak incident response capacity is not genuinely resilient—it is simply better at watching its own population. The Compass's multi-dimensional approach is designed to surface precisely these kinds of distinctions, making it harder for governments to claim accountability credit for security measures that are in tension with the rights and openness dimensions the Compass also tracks.

<h1 id="freedom-of-expression-online-measuring-what-matters">Freedom of Expression Online: Measuring What Matters</h1>

<span class="blog-date">17 March 2026 · Patryk Pawlak</span>

Freedom of expression online is one of the most contested concepts in digital governance. It sits at the intersection of human rights law, platform regulation, national security policy, and cultural politics—and every stakeholder brings a different set of assumptions about what it means and how it should be protected. For the Compass, the challenge is to measure it in a way that is both technically rigorous and normatively grounded in international human rights standards.

The rights and freedoms dimension of the Compass draws on three established indices—Freedom on the Net, the Global Cyberlaw Tracker, and the Global Index on Responsible AI—each of which captures a different facet of the digital rights landscape. Together, they provide a more complete picture than any single indicator, but they also raise difficult questions about how to aggregate incommensurable dimensions into a single score.

## The Measurement Challenge

Measuring freedom of expression online is genuinely difficult, and not only for the obvious reasons (state repression, opacity of platform moderation decisions, rapidly changing legal frameworks). It is difficult because the concept itself is contested in ways that matter for measurement. Does freedom of expression online include the right to access content regardless of national borders? Does it encompass the right to publish anonymously? How should restrictions justified on grounds of national security or public order be assessed?

The Compass takes a deliberately conservative approach to these questions, anchoring its indicators in the language of established international human rights instruments—the ICCPR, the UN Human Rights Committee's General Comments, and the Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Opinion and Expression's reports. This means the Compass will sometimes diverge from national assessments of what counts as a permissible restriction. That divergence is a feature, not a bug: the Compass is designed to provide an external reference point, not to validate national self-assessments.

## What Good Looks Like

The Compass data reveals a small but growing group of countries that are consistently performing well across the full spectrum of rights and freedoms indicators—not just on legal frameworks, but on implementation, judicial independence, and the practical ability of civil society to operate without undue interference. These countries share several characteristics: strong rule-of-law institutions, active and funded national human rights institutions with digital mandates, and a track record of constructive engagement with UN human rights mechanisms.

Importantly, high performance on rights and freedoms is positively correlated with strong performance on the other three dimensions of the Compass. Countries that protect digital rights also tend to have better connectivity, stronger resilience, and more credible responsibility frameworks. This correlation does not resolve all normative debates, but it does suggest that rights protection and broader digital development goals are more complementary than they are in tension—a finding that should inform how international support for digital governance is designed and conditioned.
